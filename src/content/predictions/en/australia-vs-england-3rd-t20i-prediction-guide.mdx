Australia and England's five-match T20I series moves to the Gabba with the score at **1-1**, which makes this third game the one that decides who takes the lead in the series. **Australia won the first match at the MCG by 3 wickets with one ball to spare**, while in the second at Carrara **England chased down 185 in just 13.1 overs** behind a 151-run opening stand. The market has England slightly ahead, but I think the bigger story is the venue: I'll look at it through the home record at the Gabba, the pitch and evening conditions, Australia's batting in the powerplay, England's openers against extra bounce, the leg-spin battle and what the odds are saying, and **I'm treating the toss as the single most important variable in this match.**

## Australia vs England, 3rd T20I: Can England's Openers Handle Chest-High Bounce?

**Start**: Friday 27 November, 18:15 local time in Brisbane (16:15 Taiwan, 08:15 GMT), The Gabba (Brisbane Cricket Ground), 20 overs a side.

**Recent form**: **the first two matches went to the side batting second.** At the MCG, England recovered from 13/2 to post 187/9, and Australia got home with 191/7 in 19.5 overs, despite dropping six catches, most of them easy ones. At Carrara the roles were reversed: Australia made 184/8, and England reached 188/2 in 13.1 overs, **Aneurin Donald hitting 85 off 32 balls with nine sixes and Phil Salt adding 58 off 30.** On attacking power alone, **England have looked the more dangerous side so far.**

**Home advantage at the Gabba**: this is where Australia's case starts. **Australia have won seven of their eight completed T20Is at the Gabba, and their only loss there was to West Indies back in 2013.** England, by contrast, have played **only one T20I at this ground, against New Zealand during the 2022 T20 World Cup** (which they won). One game is not much experience of a venue, so **on familiarity with the conditions, Australia clearly have the edge.**

**Pitch and conditions**: the Gabba's defining feature is a hard, fast surface that produces **real pace and steep bounce**, which is why it has earned the nickname "the Gabbatoir". But that same hard surface has a catch: **when there is rain around, or when the evening air turns humid, the ball loses much of its threat and bounce and the conditions swing towards the batters, which favours the side chasing.** The side batting second has won noticeably more often here in recent years, and England's chase at Carrara was also helped by the ball skidding on more easily in the evening. With the forecast showing possible thunderstorms on Friday afternoon, **I'd expect whichever captain wins the toss to bowl first**: that way they get the new ball while it is still swinging and bouncing, and get to bat later when the dew has taken the sting out of the surface.

**Australia's key: patience in the powerplay**: Australia lost the second match largely in the first six overs. **They were 58/4 at the end of the powerplay at Carrara, compared with England's 79 without loss**, so too many wickets went down to rash shots. Nikhil Chaudhary has been consistent in both matches and Australia's lower-middle order (including Jack Edwards' late hitting at the MCG) has shown real power, but that power only matters if the top order gets them there with wickets in hand. **Keeping the scoring rate up without throwing away wickets early is the main thing Australia need to get right.** ⚠️ Australia were considering changes after Carrara: Mitchell Owen could come in for Lachlan Shaw, and Billy Stanlake or Henry Thornton could be picked to make use of the Gabba's pace and bounce, while Spencer Johnson is out with a calf niggle; defer to the official team sheet.

**England's key: can they keep hitting when the ball is at chest height?** England's batting looked unstoppable at Carrara, but the Gabba asks a different question. **When the short ball is climbing to chest height, can Donald and Salt still swing through the line the way they did on the Gold Coast?** Donald described his own approach after the second match very honestly: he's "pretty decent at slogging it". On a surface with more bounce, that approach carries more risk of top edges. If England's openers get through the first few overs, though, they have already shown how quickly they can put a game out of reach. ⚠️ Will Jacks has been ruled out of the tour with a broken hand, so James Coles' left-arm spin is filling that role.

**The leg-spin battle**: **two of the best leg-spinners in white-ball cricket, Australia's Adam Zampa and England's Adil Rashid, will be bowling in the same match**, and the Gabba's long square boundaries and extra bounce can give spinners more help than people expect. Rashid is set to play his 156th T20I, moving into the top five most-capped players in the format. In a match where both batting line-ups want to go hard from the start, **whichever leg-spinner controls the middle overs better** could decide it.

**What the market is saying**: England sit at about **52.5%**, Australia about **45.9%**, with the remaining **2% or so on no result**. The market leans slightly towards England, mainly because of the strength of their batting, but the gap is small.

**Verdict**: **slight lean to England, and a much stronger lean if England win the toss and bowl first.** If Australia win the toss, bowl first and use the new ball and their knowledge of the venue to remove **Donald or Salt**, the two players who did the damage at Carrara, early on, this match is genuinely open. **But if England get to bowl first, I'd make England the side with the clear edge**, because they'd be using the conditions at their best and chasing when the surface is at its easiest to bat on.

If the rain doesn't arrive, or if the Gabba plays truer than expected in the evening, Australia's seven-from-eight home record could matter more than the toss.
