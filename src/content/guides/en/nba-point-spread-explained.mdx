An NBA point spread is a margin the book sets between the two teams: the favorite has to win by more than that number to "cover," while the underdog covers by losing by less than the number or winning outright — the aim is to bring both sides' chances of winning the bet close to even.

In [NBA Betting Basics](/en/nba/guides/nba-betting-basics/) I've already covered how to read a point spread: who's giving points, who's getting them, what the -110 in brackets means, and why spreads so often carry a half point. I won't repeat any of that here. Instead, this piece covers the forces that move a line and a few common misconceptions, so you can avoid some basic mistakes.

## The Three Main Forces That Move a Line

An NBA point spread can move a point or two between opening and tip-off, for roughly three reasons:

- **Injury news**: a star being confirmed in or out is the single biggest factor. A key player missing, or a star returning, can move the line by several points on its own — enough to flip the predicted winner.
- **Betting money flow**: when public money piles onto a favorite or a famous team, the book may shift the line toward that favorite to balance the action on each side.
- **Sharp bettors' wagers**: big-bankroll, well-informed bettors get in first, which prompts other bettors to follow, and the line gets pushed along behind them.

One thing to remember: **a line move doesn't mean the real strength of the teams changed.** Apart from the first case, the other two moves happen without any change in how good either team actually is — it's just market sentiment or the book balancing its liabilities. So there's no need to get too anxious when you see a line move, or assume someone has inside information nobody else knows. It's usually just normal movement.

## Two Common Misconceptions

**Misconception #1: the favorite just has to win.** A spread bet needs the favorite to "win by enough" — if the spread is 10.5, they have to win by 11. Say the favorite leads by twenty with three minutes left and garbage time begins: both sides pull their starters and let the end of the bench finish the game, the lead gets chipped down, and the favorite wins by only ten. That's the classic way a spread bet is lost. It's also why reading a spread isn't just about team strength — you need to look at depth, back-to-back schedules and similar details (covered in detail in the basics piece). And sometimes the bigger the gap in quality, the earlier garbage time starts and the more likely the spread is to go wrong, since for players at the end of the bench it's a rare chance to prove themselves.

**Misconception #2: the NBA has key numbers like American football.** Because of how football scores (field goals are 3 points, touchdowns 6 to 8), final margins often land on 3 and 7, which makes those numbers especially valuable. But NBA margins are distributed much more evenly, with no single whole number that comes up especially often. So **a half point is worth less**, and there's no need to pay a lot of extra juice to "buy a half point."

One more term that's easy to mix up: UK-style "spread betting" is not the same thing as the point spread discussed here. In spread betting, your payout grows or shrinks with how far off your call is, and both risk and reward are uncapped — a completely different structure from a point spread, where a win is a win and a loss is a loss. If you see the term while searching, first check which one the writer means.

## Other Spread Markets

Besides the full-game spread, books usually also offer:

- **Alternate spreads**: you choose a bigger or smaller number in exchange for different odds. For example, if the favorite is -6.5, you could pick -9.5 instead — the odds get better, but they'd need to win by more.
- **First-half or single-quarter spreads**: you bet only on the margin over one stretch of the game. Information updates fast, but there's more variance too, so it isn't ideal for beginners.

## Can Spreads From Different Leagues Be Converted?

People often ask whether there's a conversion formula between the NBA and Australia's NBL. The answer is **no**. Every league has a different game length, tempo and scoring environment — the NBA plays 48 minutes, for instance, while international rules use 40 — and simply scaling by game length is at best a rough reference point for feel, not a formula. A league's spread should be read within its own scoring environment.

## Closing

The appeal of a point spread is that it turns the too-simple question of "who wins?" into the finer question of "by how much?", and the odds are often better too. But the finer the question, the more it is affected by luck and small-probability events: garbage time, a starter fouling out, or a sudden outburst from the end of the bench can change the outcome of an entire slip. The number the book sets already reflects the collective judgment of the book and the market; what you can do is use your own insight to try to find information they don't have and so sharpen your predictions — not expect to beat them every time. All of the above is for reference only; please bet responsibly.
