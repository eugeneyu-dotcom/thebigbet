Football has the highest betting volume of any sport in the world, and because a draw is a genuine third outcome, its markets and the math behind them are more complex than most other sports. This is a complete guide to football's four main bet types, what the real numbers look like, and where to check what actually decides a match.

## Football's four main bet types, and what the real numbers look like

### 1X2 (Match Result, including the draw)

Football's most basic market — with a draw as a genuine third option, it adds a dimension that a straight two-way pick doesn't have. Shown in decimal odds:

```
Arsenal 1.65
Draw 3.80
Chelsea 5.20
```

A $100 bet on an Arsenal win returns $165 (stake included); a $100 bet on a draw returns $380; a $100 bet on a Chelsea win returns $520. The three implied probabilities add up to slightly over 100% (the excess is the bookmaker's margin) — the closer a number is to 1, the more the market favors that outcome.

### Taking the draw apart: Draw No Bet and Double Chance

1X2 treats the draw as a separate outcome, but there are two less common — yet very real — markets built on it: "Draw No Bet" and "Double Chance." And you can work out fair odds for both from the same set of prices. Using the example above (Arsenal 1.65 / Draw 3.80 / Chelsea 5.20):

| Outcome | Odds | Implied probability | Margin removed |
|------|------|----------|--------|
| Arsenal win | 1.65 | 60.6% | 57.1% |
| Draw | 3.80 | 26.3% | 24.8% |
| Chelsea win | 5.20 | 19.2% | 18.1% |
| **Total** | | **106.2%** | **100%** |

- **Draw No Bet**: if the match is drawn your stake is refunded, which takes the draw out of the picture and compares only who wins. With Arsenal at 57.1% and Chelsea at 18.1% after the margin is removed, Arsenal's chance once the draw is taken out is 57.1 ÷ (57.1 + 18.1) ≈ **75.9%**, which corresponds to fair odds (no margin) of about 1.32. That's much lower than the 1.65 in 1X2, because you no longer carry the risk of a draw.
- **Double Chance**: one bet that covers two outcomes. "Arsenal win or draw" (1X) has a probability of 57.1% + 24.8% = **81.9%**, with fair odds of about 1.22; "Chelsea win or draw" (X2) is 24.8% + 18.1% = 42.9%, with fair odds of about 2.33.
- **The difference between them**: in Double Chance a draw counts as a win, while in Draw No Bet a draw only refunds your stake — so Double Chance odds are always lower than Draw No Bet odds.

(These are "margin-free" fair odds worked out from the example prices; real platform quotes add margin on top, so they come out slightly lower. For how to strip the margin, see [Odds, Implied Probability and Expected Value](/en/strategy/odds-implied-probability-ev/).)

### Asian Handicap

A genuinely mainstream market in football, and especially popular in Asian markets — its defining feature is **using a handicap to eliminate the draw as a possible outcome entirely**. The logic is similar to a basketball point spread, but because football scores run low, Asian Handicap lines often use quarter-goal increments for finer granularity. A real line looks like this:

```
Man City -0.75 (1.90)
Tottenham +0.75 (1.95)
```

That `-0.75` is actually a combination of two separate handicap lines (-0.5 and -1, half your stake on each). Broken down:
- If City win by 1 goal: the -0.5 half wins, the -1 half pushes (refunded) → half your stake wins overall
- If City win by 2+ goals: both halves win → your full stake wins
- If City draw or lose: both halves lose → your full stake loses

This "half win" or "half win, half push" outcome is exactly what sets Asian Handicap apart from a standard whole/half-goal spread, and it's the part newcomers most often misread.

### Asian Handicap settlement table

The quarter-ball rules are admittedly harder to follow. This table sets out how the most common handicaps settle under different results (assuming you've backed the side giving the goals — the "favorite"; the headings describe the margin of victory):

| Handicap | Favorite wins by 1 | Favorite wins by 2+ | Draw | Favorite loses |
|------|------|------|------|------|
| −0.25 | Full win | Full win | Lose half | Full loss |
| −0.5 | Full win | Full win | Full loss | Full loss |
| −0.75 | Win half | Full win | Full loss | Full loss |
| −1 | Push (stake back) | Full win | Full loss | Full loss |
| −1.25 | Lose half | Full win | Full loss | Full loss |

(−0.75 is half your stake on −0.5 and half on −1; −0.25 is half on 0 and half on −0.5; −1.25 is half on −1 and half on −1.5. Other handicaps follow the same pattern.)

If you back the **underdog** instead, settlement is the mirror image:

| Handicap | Underdog loses by 1 | Draw | Underdog wins |
|------|------|------|------|
| +0.25 | Full loss | Win half | Full win |
| +0.5 | Full loss | Full win | Full win |
| +0.75 | Lose half | Full win | Full win |
| +1 | Push (stake back) | Full win | Full win |

(If the underdog loses by 2 or more, every underdog handicap above is a full loss.)

One simple rule to remember: **a line ending in .25 or .75 is always two lines with half your stake on each**, so the result can only be a full win, a full loss, or "win half / lose half" — never anything else.

### Over/Under Goals

Betting on whether the combined goals in a match (including stoppage time, but usually not a penalty shootout) go over or under a line — the most common threshold is 2.5 goals:

```
Over 2.5 goals (1.85)
Under 2.5 goals (1.95)
```

A combined total above 2.5 (i.e., 3 or more) wins the Over; 2 or fewer wins the Under. Because 2.5 is a fractional line, there's no ambiguous push scenario. Over/Under is heavily shaped by both teams' attacking style and defensive solidity — two high-pressing, leaky-defense sides tend to push totals toward the Over, while two counter-attacking, defense-first sides trend toward the Under; a large mismatch in quality also tends to push the Over.

### Over/Under has quarter-balls too — and what "full time" actually covers

Like the Asian Handicap, Over/Under also has quarter-ball thresholds such as 2.25 and 2.75, with the same logic — half your stake on each of two lines:

| Line | 1 goal or fewer | 2 goals | 3 goals | 4 goals or more |
|------|------|------|------|------|
| Over 2.25 | Full loss | Lose half | Full win | Full win |
| Over 2.5 | Full loss | Full loss | Full win | Full win |
| Over 2.75 | Full loss | Full loss | Win half | Full win |
| Over 3 | Full loss | Full loss | Push (stake back) | Full win |

For Under, read it the other way round: the fewer total goals, the better the result.

Also, **"full time" usually means 90 minutes plus stoppage time, and does not include extra time or a penalty shootout.** That matters especially in knockout games: in a match that's 1-1 after 90 minutes and only decided in extra time, Over/Under and 1X2 still look only at the 90-minute score. Rules can differ slightly between platforms, so check the platform's published rules before betting.

### Both Teams to Score (BTTS)

Betting on whether both sides score at least once in the match — it only cares whether each side scored, not how many:

```
BTTS Yes (1.75)
BTTS No (2.05)
```

This market rewards understanding both squads' setups more than the others — formation and tactical identity matter a lot here, and some setups are built for aggressive, high-pressing attack that also leaks goals at the other end. Individual player traits matter too — in an era where full-backs shape a team's identity as much as anyone, a single attack-minded, overlapping full-back can shift how a whole team plays; when both sides in a matchup have that kind of attacking full-back, BTTS Yes becomes more likely. It's related to, but not the same as, Over/Under — a 4-0 win clears the Over easily, but BTTS still settles as "No."

### Other common markets: Correct Score, Corners, Cards

Beyond these four main markets, there's also Correct Score (pick the exact final scoreline — huge odds, very low hit rate), plus Corners Over/Under and Cards Over/Under, both of which have grown more popular as corner and card statistics have become more widely tracked. The logic is identical to goals Over/Under — just swap the counted stat for corners or cards.

### Know the base rates before you read a line: this season's first six Premier League rounds

To judge whether a line is reasonable, it helps to know what the league looks like "on average." We worked through the **first six rounds of this Premier League season — 60 matches in all** — that the site tracks:

| Measure | This season's first 60 matches |
|------|------|
| Home win / draw / away win | 38.3% / 31.7% / 30.0% |
| Goals per match | 2.95 |
| Over 2.5 total goals | 56.7% |
| Both teams scored | 55.0% |

With these numbers as a baseline, you can ask a more useful question: **is this price higher or lower than the base rate?** For example, if Over 2.5 goals in a match is priced at 1.85, an implied probability of about 54%, while 56.7% of this season's Premier League matches have gone over 2.5, then — assuming nothing unusual about the two teams' styles — betting the over has relatively more value.

But two cautions:

- **60 matches is a small sample.** A home-win rate of just 38%, for instance, is far below the usual idea of home advantage, and is most likely a quirk of the first six rounds — don't treat it as settled.
- **The base rate is only a starting point.** What adjusts it are the specific teams' attacking power, defensive stability, injuries and schedule (see our [Premier League Matchweek 6 prediction guide](/en/football/leagues/predictions/epl-matchweek-6-six-clubs-prediction-guide/), where every match is broken down this way). In football especially, tactics and styles differ enormously between teams — some attack, some defend — so you can't generalize.

### Different situations, which market should you look at?

| Your read | A better-fitting market | Why |
|------|------|------|
| You back the favorite to win but fear a draw | Draw No Bet, Double Chance (1X) | A draw doesn't lose you money, at the cost of worse odds |
| You expect the favorite to win big | Asian Handicap −1 or −1.5 | They have to win by enough to count, and the price beats 1X2 |
| You expect both teams to score | BTTS "Yes", Over on goals | The two markets complement each other — BTTS means at least two goals, so going over is also relatively likely |
| You think the underdog won't lose by much | Underdog line +0.75, +1 | Losing by one goal returns your stake or loses half |
| You have no idea who'll win | Don't bet | A simple principle: no view, no bet |

That last row is meant seriously: **for a match you have no view on, the best choice is not to bet.** There are dozens of matches a week, so you'll never run short of chances.

## What actually decides football matches, and where to check it

### Injury news and confirmed lineups

A missing key player has an immediate, direct impact on a football match, especially if it's the main creative outlet or the defensive organizer. **Where to check injury news and confirm lineups**: [Sky Sports](https://www.skysports.com/football) is one of the most commonly used real-time sources for the Premier League and Europe's major leagues; the official starting lineup is usually posted by both clubs' official social accounts around an hour before kickoff — that's the fastest, most authoritative first-hand source.

### Fixture congestion and rotation

With the Champions League, Europa League and domestic league schedules overlapping, top clubs often play two or even three matches a week — that "fixture congestion" directly affects starting-lineup rotation, and a key player coming off a tough European fixture is more likely to be rested or running on tired legs for the following league match. Tracking a team's recent fixture density, and where a given match falls in that schedule (e.g., right before or after a Champions League knockout leg), is the key to judging rotation risk. Squad depth matters here too — a club like this season's Arsenal, who can field two genuinely competitive lineups, is much less exposed to rotation risk than a squad without that depth.

### Home advantage

Football's home advantage is about as pronounced as it gets among major sports — long-run home win rates typically sit between 45% and 50% (versus roughly 25% each for a draw or an away win), and crowd atmosphere, pitch familiarity, and skipping the travel fatigue of an away trip are all real, tangible factors worth weighting heavily.

### Referee tendencies

Different referees have genuinely different standards for what counts as a foul and how quickly they reach for a card, and that has a real impact on the handicap, corners, and cards markets — a referee known for stricter enforcement tends to push the cards market toward the Over, while a referee who lets the game flow more (and calls fewer fouls) can suppress corner counts, since a more flowing game with fewer stoppages in possession tends to produce fewer corners. Once the referee assignment is announced, it's worth checking that official's average cards and fouls-called numbers for the season so far — most football stats sites carry a dedicated historical page for individual referees.

## A risk unique to football betting: fixing and manipulation concerns

One of football's most notorious manipulation cases happened in England in 1964 — **the Sheffield Wednesday scandal, where three players deliberately threw a match**. Former Scotland youth international [Jimmy Gauld](wiki:Jimmy_Gauld) organized a betting ring after his playing career ended, and in 1962 used inside information from Sheffield Wednesday player [David Layne](wiki:David_Layne) to target the club's away fixture at Ipswich Town on December 1st — Layne judged the team was likely to lose that day, and brought in teammates [Peter Swan](wiki:Peter_Swan_%28footballer%2C_born_1936%29) and [Tony Kay](wiki:Tony_Kay) to bet on the loss together and throw the match. Wednesday lost 0-2, exactly as the fix had called for.

Once the scandal was exposed by *The Sunday People*, all three were banned from football for life and served prison time — at the time, both Swan and Kay were England international-caliber players, and the scandal is widely believed to have cost Swan a place in England's 1966 World Cup-winning squad. The bans were eventually lifted in 1972, but by then both players' peak years were already gone.

The case illustrates a point: **team-level match-fixing needs several players to conspire together, which makes it harder to hide and easier to get caught by than a single player manipulating their own individual stats — unnatural-looking team performance draws attention.** (Swan himself later admitted, "we lost the game fair and square, but I still don't know what I'd have done if we'd been winning" — even from inside the fix, acting against the basic logic of competition was clearly hard to sustain psychologically.) But because it requires several people to coordinate, when team-level fixing does happen, the scale of the damage tends to be larger than a single player's manipulation.

## Bottom line

Before you look at a football line, first work out whether it's 1X2, an Asian Handicap, Over/Under, or BTTS — the Asian Handicap's half-win mechanic in particular is where newcomers most often misjudge things. Layer in injury news, fixture-congestion rotation risk, home advantage, and referee tendencies, and you'll get a much better read on where a match is headed than the odds number alone.

Look back at the numbers in this piece: the same Arsenal price of 1.65 is only 57% once the margin is removed; take the draw out and it's 76%; and in the first six rounds of this Premier League season, home teams won just 38% of matches. All of this is a reminder that football results carry a great deal of built-in uncertainty. Football has many variables and no shortage of upsets — bet responsibly and manage your stake size.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do extra time and penalty shootouts count in the markets?**
On most platforms, 1X2, Asian Handicap and Over/Under all look only at 90 minutes plus stoppage time. To include extra time or progression, you usually need to pick a "to qualify" market or one the platform labels as "including extra time" — follow each platform's own rules.

**Why do different platforms set different Over/Under lines for the same match?**
Each platform sets the line at 2.25, 2.5 or 2.75 according to its own risk management, then compensates through the odds. When the line differs, the odds on both sides adjust with it, so compare "line plus odds" together rather than the line alone.

**What's the difference between the Asian Handicap and the European handicap?**
The Asian Handicap has only two outcomes — the draw is removed (pushed and refunded). The European handicap still has three outcomes, and a draw after the handicap is applied is its own separate option. This site covers the Asian Handicap.

**Which market should I learn first?**
Start with 1X2, then Draw No Bet, and only then the Asian Handicap. Getting a firm grip on stripping the margin and implied probability (see [this piece](/en/strategy/odds-implied-probability-ev/)) matters more than rushing to learn every market.
