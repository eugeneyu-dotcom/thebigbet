Group standings look simple until two teams end up with the exact same number of points. Then suddenly you're squinting at goal difference, wondering if a card picked up in the second group match actually matters. It does — fair play points are a real tiebreaker, and they've decided a World Cup spot before. Let me walk through the full order FIFA has used in recent tournaments (this format has been standard since the 2018 World Cup) for breaking a tie between two teams in the same group.

It's worth being precise about scope here, because it's easy to mix this up with a different rule: this is about ranking two teams **within the same group** who are level on points. It's a different question from ranking the best third-placed teams **across different groups**, which only uses points, goal difference, and goals scored — because teams from different groups never actually play each other, so head-to-head comparisons aren't possible there. If that's the question you're after, I cover it in my [2026 World Cup qualification rules explainer](/en/football/world-cup/guides/2026-world-cup-qualification-rules).

## Step 1-3: Points, Goal Difference, Goals Scored

Before any tiebreaker kicks in, the basics apply across all group matches:

1. **Points (PTS)**
2. **Goal Difference (GD)**
3. **Goals Scored (GS)**

Most of the time, this settles things on its own. The tiebreakers below only matter when two teams are still deadlocked after all three.

## Step 4-5: Head-to-Head Result and Head-to-Head Goal Difference

If teams are still level, the next check isn't the overall group table — it's just the match(es) played directly between the tied teams.

4. **Points earned in the head-to-head meeting(s)**
5. **Goal difference in the head-to-head meeting(s)**

So if Team A beat Team B in the group stage, and every other metric between them is identical, Team A wins the tiebreaker outright — that single result overrides the rest of the group table comparison. This is intuitive once you think about it: it's a direct answer to "who's actually better between these two," rather than an indirect one built from games against other opponents.

## Step 6: Fair Play Points

This is the one that surprises people, but it's a genuine part of the regulations: if teams are still tied after head-to-head, disciplinary record breaks it. Fair play points are calculated from cautions and dismissals across the group stage — yellow cards, second yellows, and straight reds each cost points, and the team with the *better* (lower) fair-play score gets the tiebreaker.

This isn't a hypothetical rule. It's genuinely decided a World Cup outcome: at the 2018 World Cup, Japan advanced over Senegal out of Group H after the two finished level on points, goal difference, goals scored, and head-to-head — Japan's cleaner disciplinary record settled it. It remains the clearest real-world proof that fair play points aren't just a footnote — they can be the difference between advancing and going home.

## Step 7: Drawing of Lots

If, somehow, two teams are still tied after all of the above, FIFA falls back to a drawing of lots — essentially a coin-flip-style random draw conducted by FIFA officials. It's the tiebreaker of last resort, precisely because it's the only one left once every measurable performance metric has been exhausted, and in practice it's vanishingly rare for a match to actually reach this stage.

## Why the Order Matters

The sequence isn't arbitrary — it moves from the most informative comparison to the least. Points and goal difference measure overall group performance; head-to-head asks the more targeted question of who beat whom directly; fair play points step in only when performance genuinely can't separate two sides; and lots exist purely to guarantee the competition always produces a decisive answer, however unsatisfying a coin flip might feel as a way to end a World Cup campaign.

Next time you see two teams still level heading into the final round of group matches, this is the order to watch: don't just check the table position, check whether they've already played each other, and keep an eye on the disciplinary count too — a needless late tackle in a dead rubber can matter more than it looks like it should.
