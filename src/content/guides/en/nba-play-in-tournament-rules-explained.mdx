Back in the day, the rule for reaching the NBA playoffs was simple: once the regular season wrapped up, the top 8 teams by conference record went through. But that created a problem — once a team's playoff position outside the very edge of the 8-9 cutoff was basically decided, the rest of its games turned into dead rubbers, both sides resting starters and playing bench units, and the quality of basketball dropped off a cliff. That's why, starting in the 2020-21 season, the league added a Play-In Tournament after the regular season, where the No. 7 through No. 10 seeds in each conference fight it out for the final two playoff spots (the No. 7 and No. 8 seeds). Suddenly a lot of teams couldn't afford to coast through the stretch run — teams sitting at No. 5 or No. 6 had to keep winning to stay clear of the play-in zone, while teams around No. 10 through No. 12 had real incentive to fight for every last game just to get a shot at qualifying. Late-season basketball got a lot more watchable, fans responded well, and the rule has stuck around ever since.

## Round 1: 7 vs. 8, 9 vs. 10

Two games are played at the same time in the first round:
- **No. 7 hosts No. 8**: the winner locks in the No. 7 playoff seed outright; the loser gets one more chance (see below)
- **No. 9 hosts No. 10**: the winner advances to the final play-in game; the loser is eliminated, no second chance

## Round 2 (the final): loser of 7-vs-8 hosts winner of 9-vs-10

The team that lost the 7-vs-8 game hosts the winner of the 9-vs-10 game, and the winner of that game takes the final playoff spot — the No. 8 seed.

In other words, whichever of the No. 7 or No. 8 seed loses game one still gets one shot at revival — win either of their two games and you're in. The No. 9 and No. 10 seeds, on the other hand, only get one life each: lose the 9-vs-10 game and the season is over. Home court in every game goes to the higher seed.

## This rule has genuinely decided teams' fates

Since it started in 2020-21, the Play-In Tournament has proven its worth — beyond making the stretch run more watchable, it's produced no shortage of its own legendary moments. In 2022-23, the Miami Heat lost their first play-in game to the Hawks, then barely scraped into the playoffs as the No. 8 seed by beating the Bulls — and then eliminated the No. 1-seed Milwaukee Bucks, who had finished the regular season with a far better record, 4-1 in the first round. In 2024-25, the Heat went even further: the first-ever No. 10 seed to fight through the play-in and reach the playoffs, with Tyler Herro dropping 30 points in the deciding game. Nobody's ridden the play-in all the way to a championship yet — but maybe one day. It's hard not to call this one of the genuinely good changes the NBA has made in recent years.

## Why the asymmetry is designed this way

The logic here is pretty intuitive: a team that earned a better regular-season record should get some real advantage for it — otherwise, what was the point of grinding out all those extra wins? Giving the No. 7 and No. 8 seeds two shots, both with home court against the lower seeds, is a fair way to reflect that gap. A No. 9 or No. 10 seed fighting for a playoff spot, on the other hand, has to prove it actually belongs there, so it's fair that the bar is set higher.

If you're a longtime fan just getting back into the league, don't write off a team sitting between No. 9 and No. 10 in the standings as "not making the playoffs" — until both play-in rounds are done, anyone in that range still has a shot. And if a No. 10 seed actually fights all the way through, that's exactly the underdog story worth watching.
